Abstract
Low nutritional literacy and the rising burden of non-communicable diseases create a need for practical tools that help people interpret everyday food information. This study develops and evaluates Healthier, a nutrition-assistance application that integrates plate-image segmentation, nutrition-label extraction, and a deterministic Clinical Rules Engine with Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) verification. A YOLO11s-seg model was trained on public datasets (11,684 training and 2,017 validation images) to partition plates into five Isi Piringku groups, and was tested on three held-out sets of 5,013 images that were never used for training or model selection. Nutrition tables were read with PaddleOCR (PP-OCRv4) and a grid-based layout parser, and evaluated with field-level accuracy and image-level cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals. The segmentation model reached a mask mAP50 of 71.66% on validation, 69.15% on UECFoodPIXCOMPLETE, and 63.66% on FoodSeg103, but only 17.06% on the cross-domain FruitSeg30, indicating a measurable domain shift. In a six-image pilot, the grid parser raised field-level accuracy from 21.74% to 47.83%. The rules engine matched its specification in all 66 checks, whereas label-extraction errors changed the clinical status in 39–50% of pilot decisions, which supports retaining user verification. These findings show the feasibility of a transparent, guideline-aligned workflow and make its generalisation limits explicit.

INTRODUCTION
Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are strongly associated with dietary patterns, and diabetes and hypertension remain among the largest contributors to the disease burden in Indonesia. The 2023 Indonesian Health Survey (SKI) reported a diabetes prevalence of 11.7%, an increase from 10.9% in the 2018 Basic Health Research (Riskesdas), while hypertension prevalence decreased from 34.1% to 30.8% over the same period   Analyses of the 2023 SKI further link the increase in diabetes cases to modifiable lifestyle and metabolic factors such as central obesity and infrequent blood-pressure screening. Because a substantial share of diagnosed individuals do not maintain regular treatment or follow-up ,  practical tools that support everyday self-management are needed alongside clinical care.
At the individual level, the ability to interpret nutrition information is limited. A systematic review of traffic-light nutrition labelling in Indonesia reported that consumer understanding of nutrition labels remains low, even though attitudes toward simplified label formats are generally positive . New front-of-pack initiatives such as the Nutri-Level label have been introduced with the same objective of helping consumers choose healthier products . In practice, however, conventional nutrition tables are difficult to process automatically because the text can be small, layouts vary across products, and reflections or curved packaging degrade image quality . For meals served on a plate there is no printed table at all, so dietary assessment must begin from visual food recognition.
Image-based dietary assessment is usually described as a pipeline of food image analysis, volume or portion estimation, and nutrient derivation. Reviews of the field note that segmentation, recognition, and nutrient estimation are commonly developed as separate tasks, and that dataset diversity, portion estimation, and real-world image conditions remain persistent challenges  Indonesian contributions remain scarce: most existing work focuses on classification of local dishes rather than instance segmentation of plated meals . For example, Rasyidi et al introduced a classification dataset of 24,427 images covering 160 Indonesian food types, but to the best of our knowledge there is still no public instance-segmentation benchmark for Indonesian plated meals. Generalisation across sources is a known concern as well: recent food-video segmentation benchmarks show that current methods suffer from limited data diversity and degrade under new viewpoints and domains . Domain shift must therefore be measured explicitly rather than assumed away.
The label-reading problem presents a parallel challenge. A benchmark on real-world food packaging compared Tesseract, EasyOCR, PaddleOCR, and TrOCR, and found that dense tabular structures such as nutrition-facts panels are a major source of recognition errors . For this reason, preprocessing, layout reconstruction, and domain-specific correction are commonly required in Indonesian settings . PaddleOCR provides an Apache-licensed OCR toolkit that includes the PP-OCR model family and is practical for local deployment .
Automated recognition still makes mistakes, so human verification matters. In a longitudinal food-logging study, image recognition still required a 20.7% log edit rate, showing that users remain an essential part of the loop . This motivates the Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) design adopted in Healthier, where users can correct segmentation and extracted values before they are used by the decision layer. Local inference is also attractive for health applications because on-device processing can reduce privacy exposure and dependence on continuous cloud connectivity .
The literature provides strong evidence for individual components, but relatively few studies integrate plate-based food analysis, packaged-label extraction, and deterministic guideline-based decision-making into a single offline workflow with explicit human verification. Healthier addresses this integration gap through three research questions: (RQ1) how well a segmentation model trained on non-Indonesian food datasets generalises to new and unseen sources, and which classes fail (RQ2) to what extent a layout-aware OCR pipeline improves nutrition-table extraction over a baseline parser, and what error modes remain; and (RQ3) how segmentation and label outputs can be translated into transparent, guideline-aligned feedback with explicit user verification.
This study makes three explicit contributions, each stated together with how it is demonstrated experimentally, so that the novelty rests on a verifiable integration and decision mechanism rather than on merely combining existing technologies. First, a leakage-controlled evaluation protocol for cross-source food segmentation: we define a data protocol with perceptual-hash deduplication, a fixed validation split, and three independent external test sets that are never used for training or model selection, and we report per-class generalisation across sources (evidenced by the internal and external results and the per-class error analysis in the Results and Discussion).
Second, a reproducible, layout-aware OCR pipeline for nutrition tables we combine four preprocessing stages with a grid-based table parser and fuzzy field mapping, and evaluate it at the field level with image-level cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals, together with a per-stage ablation design. Third, a deterministic, guideline-grounded decision layer with HITL verification we translate verified segmentation and label values into SAFE/WARNING/DANGER feedback using explicit thresholds derived from PERKENI 2024 and hypertension guidance, while retaining an explicit user-correction step. The novelty is therefore in the integration mechanism, its validation protocol, and its transparent decision strategy, all of which are specified and testable, rather than in a claim that any single model is state of the art.
RESEARCH METHODS
This research adopts an iterative experimental methodology informed by CRISP-ML(Q), with separate evaluation of the segmentation, OCR, clinical-rule, and usability components. The design emphasises repeated validation and strict separation of data because variability in food images, datasets, ground truth, and reporting practices is a major barrier to reliable comparison between dietary-assessment systems .
Types of Research
This is applied, quantitative, and experimental research. It develops an engineering artefact (the Healthier pipeline) and evaluates it with objective metrics rather than subjective judgement. The study is descriptive for the system design and comparative for the component evaluation the design compares each component against a baseline or an alternative configuration. The experimental design is a controlled component-wise evaluation: the data split is fixed once and reused by every experiment, and each module (segmentation, OCR, clinical rules) is evaluated independently before being integrated.
Time and Place of Research
The research was carried out between June 2026 and November 2026 at the Informatics Engineering Study Program, Universitas Krisnadwipayana, Jakarta, Indonesia. Model training used a cloud Kaggle Tesla T4 GPU environment, while OCR experiments and interface checks ran locally on a laptop with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 Laptop GPU (6 GB) and a CPU PyTorch build, matching the intended offline deployment condition.
Research Target / Subject
The study targets two families of objects and one target user profile. The segmentation target consists of plated-food images; the evaluation subjects are three public image datasets (UECFoodPIXCOMPLETE, FoodSeg103, and FruitSeg30) whose original annotations were reused. The OCR target consists of photographs of Indonesian packaged-snack nutrition labels; the evaluation subject was a pilot set of six labels with hand-transcribed ground truth. The clinical target is everyday users with diet-related NCD risk profiles (type-2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and diabetic nephropathy). Sampling for the public datasets followed the official splits; label photographs were a convenience sample of packaged snacks available in local stores.
Procedure
The end-to-end procedure has two input paths that share one verification layer and one decision layer. A plate photograph is segmented into food instances and grouped into five Isi Piringku groups (Staple Food, Side Dishes, Vegetables, Fruits, Others); a packaging photograph is preprocessed, recognised, and parsed into nutrition fields. Both outputs pass through a HITL verification layer and are then converted by the deterministic Clinical Rules Engine into SAFE/WARNING/DANGER feedback.
Figure 1 summarises the workflow
Figure 1 summarises the workflow of Healthier. A plate photograph is processed by a YOLO11s-seg network, which predicts food instances and their masks; the instances are grouped into the five Isi Piringku categories, and the proportion of each group is computed from polygon areas (Equation 1). A nutrition-label photograph is first preprocessed (EXIF orientation, deskewing, deglare, CLAHE, and ROI cropping) and then passed to PaddleOCR PP-OCRv4 for text detection and recognition. A layout-reconstruction stage, consisting of a semantic filter, fuzzy field mapping (similarity ≥ 0.75), and a grid parser, converts the recognised text into per-serving nutrient fields. Both outputs are verified by the user in the Human-in-the-Loop step and then evaluated by the Clinical Rules Engine, which uses BMR/TDEE-based limits to return SAFE, WARNING, or DANGER feedback.
Dataset Construction, Annotation, and Splits
Three public datasets were used: UECFoodPIXCOMPLETE , FoodSeg103 , and FruitSeg30 . FruitSeg30 contains 1,969 images in 30 fruit classes collected in natural and uncontrolled environments across Malaysia, Bangladesh, and Australia . The candidate corpus contained 19,087 images. Duplicate images were detected with a difference hash (dHash) computed on an 8×8 grayscale reduction of each image, and 373 duplicates were removed, leaving 18,714 unique images. Class labels from all sources were remapped into the five Isi Piringku groups using a fixed mapping table.
We did not re-annotate the public masks; the original annotations were used as provided, and only the label remapping was applied. A stratified audit of the remapping (200 sampled images, 40 per class, by two annotators, reporting Cohen's κ and resolving disagreements by discussion) is recommended to document this step; it is not reported here and remains future work.
The training pool consisted of the official training portions of UEC and FoodSeg103 and was split 85:15 into training (11,684) and validation (2,017) sets, stratified by class with seed 42 and no overlap. Validation images are therefore a held-out 15% of the training pool. Three sets were reserved exclusively for external testing and were never used for training, early stopping, or checkpoint selection: the official UEC test set (948 images), the FoodSeg103 validation set (2,096 images), and the whole of FruitSeg30 (1,969 images), for 5,013 external images in total. Because the Fruits class in the training data comes only from FoodSeg103, FruitSeg30 acts as a deliberate domain-shift test. No oversampling and no class-weighted loss were applied, so the external results are not confounded by resampling. Segmentation Model and Training
The model is YOLO11s-seg (Khanam & Hussain, 2024). It was trained with Ultralytics 8.4.199 for at most 100 epochs with early-stopping patience 20; training stopped at epoch 67 and the best checkpoint was epoch 48. Optimisation used AdamW with an initial learning rate of 0.001, a final learning-rate factor of 0.01, weight decay 0.0005, and momentum 0.9, with batch size 16, image size 640, and seed 42 (deterministic). Spatial and photometric augmentation was applied (HSV jitter, translation, scaling, horizontal flipping, mosaic, and random erasing). Training took 4.01 h. Table 3 lists the full configuration.

Nutrition-Label Extraction Pipeline
Packaging images undergo four preprocessing stages before recognition, including deskewing using the Hough Transform   and glare removal through Telea inpainting , supported by recent specular highlight removal approaches . CLAHE-based contrast normalisation, and region-of-interest cropping around the nutrition table. The processed region is recognised with PaddleOCR . configured with the PP-OCRv4 recognition model.
Layout reconstruction then (i) groups text boxes into logical rows using Y-band grouping; (ii) detects the value columns (Per Sajian / Serving and %AKG / %DV) relative to the row-label column; and (iii) splits tall numeric boxes that PP-OCR merges across adjacent rows (for example, 1725 is split into 1, 7, 2, 5), thereby separating the per-serving and %AKG columns. A semantic filter removes non-nutritional text (promotional slogans, BPOM registration numbers, ingredient lists, and cooking instructions). Finally, fuzzy matching based on edit distance maps OCR tokens to standardised nutrition terms; the Levenshtein distance and similarity are given in Equations 2 to 4.
Clinical Rules Engine
Nutrition values that have passed HITL verification are evaluated by a deterministic rules engine that runs locally in the browser (no cloud inference). The engine computes Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) with the Mifflin–St Jeor equations and Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) as BMR multiplied by an activity factor (Equations 5 to 7).
where W is body weight (kg), H is height (cm), A is age (years), and AF is the activity factor (1.2, the sedentary value used by the application). Personal limits are then derived per profile (Table 4). For type-2 diabetes, the daily sugar limit follows PERKENI 2024 and is set dynamically at 5% of TDEE (0.05 × TDEE / 4 g); for non-diabetic profiles the default is 10% of TDEE (0.10 × TDEE / 4 g). Sodium limits are absolute values: 1,500 mg/day for hypertension and 2,300 mg/day otherwise. Saturated-fat limits are 10% of TDEE (0.10 × TDEE / 9 g), reduced to 7% (0.07 × TDEE / 9 g) for diabetes with dyslipidaemia; cholesterol is limited to 200 mg for diabetes with dyslipidaemia; and the protein limit for diabetic nephropathy is 0.8 g × body weight (PERKENI, 2024; James et al., 2014). All limits are implemented exactly as listed in Table 4.

The engine classifies accumulated intake into SAFE, WARNING, and DANGER (Table 5). A WARNING is raised when sugar or saturated fat exceeds 50% of the daily limit, when actual sodium exceeds 40% of the sodium limit, when protein exceeds 30% of the nephropathy quota, or when a product's sodium density exceeds 600 mg/100 g for hypertension (800 mg/100 g otherwise). DANGER is raised only when sugar exceeds 100% of the daily limit; sodium and saturated fat saturate at WARNING. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
This section reports the quantitative evaluation of the two recognition modules and their behaviour under domain shift, together with the component ablation, baseline, and integration analyses where results are available; the analyses that remain to be completed are marked explicitly.
Instance Segmentation Evaluation
On the 2,017-image internal validation split (best checkpoint, epoch 48), the YOLO11s-seg model reached a mask mAP50 of 71.66% and a mask mAP50-95 of 56.91%, with mask precision 72.95% and recall 65.50%. Box metrics were slightly higher: box mAP50 72.37%, box mAP50-95 60.11%, box precision 72.87%, and box recall 66.04% (Table 6). The gap between precision and recall is expected in instance segmentation of plated food, where overlapping and heterogeneous regions make some instances easier to localise than to recall fully; this recall gap is one reason the system retains an interactive correction step. Generalisation to External Sources and Per-Class Error Analysis
Because the training data are not Indonesian-specific, external evaluation is the central generalisation test. The model was applied unchanged to the three held-out sources. Mask mAP50 was 69.15% on UECFoodPIXCOMPLETE test and 63.66% on the FoodSeg103 validation set   a drop of only a few points relative to internal validation   but fell to 17.06% on FruitSeg30 (Table 7). Because the training and evaluation pipeline, classes, and metrics were identical across sources, the drop isolates a domain shift rather than a training error. The pattern is interpretable. UEC and FoodSeg103 contain plated meals and kitchen food photographs that share low-level statistics with the training pool, whereas FruitSeg30 contains isolated fruit in natural, uncontrolled environments (Shamrat et al., 2024) a different visual domain that the model was never trained on, especially because the Fruits class in training comes only from FoodSeg103. We therefore treat the FruitSeg30 result as an explicit limitation and as evidence for the generalisation and failure-analysis contribution, not as a headline strength.
Baseline Comparison
A full-budget comparison against an alternative segmentation architecture could not be completed: the main model was trained on a cloud T4 GPU, whereas the local environment available for this study had a CPU-only PyTorch build, so retraining an alternative architecture with the same 100-epoch budget and split was not feasible within the study window. A short five-epoch screening run with three alternative architectures is available from an earlier notebook: mask mAP50 was 54.67% for YOLOv8m-seg, 52.93% for YOLO11m-seg, and 53.45% for YOLO26m-seg (box mAP50 56.27%, 54.58%, and 54.89%, respectively). Because that run used a reduced training subset and only five epochs, these numbers are reported only as a screening pilot; they are not comparable to the 100-epoch YOLO11s-seg model (internal mask mAP50 71.66%) and must not be read as evidence that any architecture is superior. A matched full-budget baseline remains future work.
Nutrition-Label Extraction and Preprocessing Ablation
The OCR module was evaluated with field-level accuracy on Indonesian snack-label photographs with hand-transcribed ground truth. This evaluation is an exploratory pilot on six images (46 fields); it is reported to document the effect of the layout parser and is not a generalisation result. The baseline parser achieved 21.74% field-level accuracy (95% cluster-bootstrap CI [16.67%, 25.58%]), and the revised grid parser achieved 47.83% (95% CI [29.17%, 66.67%])   more than double   with missing fields falling from 65.22% to 34.78% of the reference fields (Table 10).

The dominant failure mode of the baseline was coverage rather than mis-recognition: missing fields accounted for 65.22% of the reference fields because PP-OCR merged the numeric grid and the parser failed to detect several rows, so most errors were absent values rather than wrong values. Residual errors concentrate on wrong-value assignment (for example, a mis-parsed sugar value with a very large relative error) and on serving-size formatting and distinguishing per-serving from per-100 g values. Discussion
The evaluation demonstrates strong supervised in-domain performance while quantifying the impact of domain shifts, consistent with existing benchmarks on data diversity and viewpoint degradation  . When reading labels on dense Indonesian tables, the main bottleneck stems from layout reconstruction rather than raw OCR accuracy, supporting previous findings on complex tabular structures   and the necessity of layout-aware corrections . consequently, current OCR findings serve as a pilot phase that demands larger, brand-stratified validation. Systemically, the architecture merges plate recognition and packaged-label extraction to feed a local, deterministic decision pipeline incorporating human-in-the-loop verification. Key threats to validity include the limited, unstratified OCR dataset, single-seed internal validation, and a screening-only segmentation baseline. Furthermore, error propagation analysis revealed that label extraction mistakes alter 39–50% of automated pilot decision sunderscoring the necessity of user verification  while the decision rules reflect functional rather than clinical validation without dietitian oversight, supported by an exploratory two-evaluator usability panel and constrained by the lack of a public Indonesian plate-segmentation benchmark.
CONCLUSIONS AND SUGGESTIONS
This chapter presents the primary conclusions drawn from the experimental evaluation and model performance analysis. Additionally, it outlines strategic recommendations and prospective directions for future research to address existing limitations and enhance overall system capability.
Conclusion
This research evaluated Healthier, an edge-oriented nutrition system integrating YOLO11s-seg plate segmentation, PaddleOCR (PP-OCRv4) label extraction, a deterministic Clinical Rules Engine, and HITL verification. Internal validation achieved a mask mAP50 of 71.66% (mAP50-95 56.91%). External sources retained mask mAP50 at 69.15% (UECFoodPIXCOMPLETE) and 63.66% (FoodSeg103), but dropped to 17.06% on FruitSeg30, explicitly framing domain shift as a limitation. For nutrition labels, an exploratory six-image pilot showed a grid-based layout parser doubled field-level accuracy over baseline (21.74% → 47.83%), identifying layout reconstruction as the primary bottleneck. The rule layer was functionally verified (36/36 daily limits; 30/30 status decisions matching), while propagation tests showed label errors altered clinical status in 39–50% of pilot decisions, justifying the HITL verification step. Heuristic evaluation found no severity-4 issues (severity 2–3 only). Limitations include a small OCR sample, 2D portion estimation, and performance drop on the heterogeneous "Others" class. Overall, combining deterministic rules with HITL controls enables transparent, guideline-aligned feedback while defining clear generalization boundaries..
Suggestion
Four directions are recommended for future work: building a public Indonesian plate-segmentation and nutrition-label benchmark with texturally complex foods; improving cross-domain robustness and portion estimation beyond 2D area ratios via depth or multi-angle imaging; expanding OCR evaluation to at least 100 brand-stratified images with a full preprocessing ablation and applying edge quantization alongside an optional offline small language model for explanation strictly as a subordinate component that cannot override deterministic rules or human verification. Finally, prospective studies with end users and nutrition professionals are needed to evaluate clinical utility.
